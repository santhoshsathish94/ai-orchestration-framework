Clover

A five-leaf framework of using AI.

A team runs four stages, and it runs them on the systems it already has: Direction, Context, Action, Success. Growth is the fifth. It belongs to the frontier AI companies and to the volume of data everyone's usage generates, so it is the next stage rather than one anybody runs today.

Direction
The human controls what needs to be done, decides how it should be done, and approves.
Context
Everything the work reasons from. At its best, the systems the organization already runs.
Action
AI plans the work, does it, tests it, and iterates.
Success
The real environment shows the intended outcome.
Growth
What AI learns out of the other four stages. The next stage, and not one a team runs.

Where AI already is

AI current capabilities

AI is already past what any individual human can do. It holds the collection of skills humans have, in one place.

Engineering
Reads code it has never seen, explains how the system behaves, and writes changes that fit the way the project is already built.
Quality assurance
Designs cases from how the system behaves rather than from the wording of the ticket.
Security review
Reads a change for the class of mistake that causes incidents, and reads a repository for the credential nobody meant to commit.
Analysis
Reconstructs what happened from logs and telemetry, fast enough to matter while an incident is still open.

What it does not have is direction, and the will to act on its own. Someone has to say what is worth doing, and someone has to start it. The rest of this page is about that.

Three leaves

The common clover

This is how AI is used almost everywhere today. A human gives the Direction. AI performs the Action, working from what that one human can hand over. After several passes the result becomes Success.

Direction
The human controls what needs to be done, decides how it should be done, and approves. Control stays with the human.
Action
AI plans the work, writes it, tests it, and brings back something to look at.
Success
The human reads the result, corrects it, and runs the loop again until it is good enough.

A lot of real value comes out of this, and it is ordinary. The limit is Context. Everything the work reasons from has to come from one human. That is more than what they type: they can attach files, or point at the repository they happen to be working in. It is still only what that one human can reach and remember.

What happens there

Working from a thin description, AI produces the plausible answer: something that reads correctly and describes a system that does not exist, and searching for the exact string it cited usually settles it. Constraints given once in a long session quietly stop being honored, because the only place they ever lived was the conversation. Then comes the thrash, where a change fails and the next attempt is a variation of the same change, each one as confident as the last, with no new information arriving between the two. Underneath all of it, motion gets read as progress. AI removes the effort cost of starting work and leaves the attention cost of directing it, so human attention stays the scarce resource.

Four leaves

The lucky clover

Context is the fourth stage, and it is what the framework exists for. Context stops being the handful of things one human can hand over. It becomes the systems the organization already runs.

This is also the whole of the working loop: Direction, Context, Action, Success. Four stages, run by a team, on systems the team already has.

What context is now

  • Every repository the team works in, with its many projects and their documentation.
  • The datasources the applications connect to.
  • The logs and telemetry those applications produce.
  • The deployment environments.
  • The running applications and websites.

None of it has to be written out first. It is already there, and it is already true, which is more than can be said for most descriptions of it.

How to set it up

Three moves, one system at a time. None of them creates a new credential, and none of them needs a platform to migrate onto first.

The build sequence in full →

1 · Put read-only MCP servers in front
An agent reaches a system through an MCP server that only reads. Stand one up per system, one at a time, starting with the repository the team spends its days in.
2 · Scope it to the human
Each connection is scoped to what the human already has access to, at the privileges they already hold. No new credential, and no new path into the systems.
3 · Start in development
One environment is enough to begin, and development is enough. Widen to the other non-production environments as the results hold up.

Opening systems to an agent gets challenged, and the challenge deserves a straight answer.

That access already exists and is already being used. Repositories get cloned, logs get read, and datasources get queried every day, often without anyone tracking who reached what. Clover makes the same access deliberate, scoped and visible.

Reading across those systems also surfaces problems while they are still cheap. Stale credentials, access paths nobody has reviewed, data nobody has looked at in years. They come up in the first weeks of reading, well before they turn into incidents.

The needle and the haystack

Connecting all of it does not finish the job. An organization's systems are a haystack, and the thing worth finding is a needle somewhere in it. Expecting AI to search the whole haystack does not work, and it costs a lot to watch it try.

That is what changes Direction. The humans who work on the system every day can point at roughly where the needle fell. They know which service went out last week, which job has always been fragile, and which team owns the part nobody writes down. So Direction says which part of the system to read first, rather than describing the task in detail.

Direction that points, together with context that is real, is what produces Success worth having. Both parts already exist in most organizations, which is why this is reachable for ordinary teams and not only for the ones with a research budget.

Every pass adds context

One pass rarely finishes anything. What comes back from the first pass is context for the second: the file that turned out to matter, the log line nobody had read, the theory that died. Each pass is better because the context is better. Running the same request again on the same information lands in the same place.

After each success or failure, the context files are written before the next attempt. Iteration feeds Context, and it is the only stage it feeds. Nothing a team learns in a cycle feeds Growth.

Markdown files kept beside the work are a plain way to hold that. They carry the goal, what is settled, what remains, and what was ruled out, and they get reviewed with the change like any other file. The summary in them is what matters most. With it written down, no single agent has to hold the job. Any agent can read it and carry on, and so can a colleague who picks the work up on Monday.

What the file holds

The goal
What outcome is being pursued, and what done means.
Established
Findings that are settled, and the evidence behind each.
Remaining
Open questions, unverified assumptions, next steps.
Ruled out
What was tried and failed, and why. This is the most expensive thing to rediscover.

Spend capability where it counts

A capable agent holds the plan and owns the outcome. Cheaper ones do scoped work beneath it as subagents, and the owning agent checks and corrects what comes back.

That costs far less than running everything at the top tier, and it holds up better than running everything at the bottom, because accountability stays in one place. How to divide the work →

Who holds what

Human
Access and judgment. Which systems may be read, which data may be used, where the needle probably fell, and when the context in hand is good enough to act on.
AI
Most of the gathering. Reading code, tracing a call path, pulling telemetry, reconstructing an incident from logs, finding the earlier attempt nobody remembered. It also reports what it could not reach, and that part matters most.

What happens there

Hoarding. A context stuffed with everything reachable is harder to reason from than a context with the right few things in it.

A gap filled with a guess. A gap an agent names is a limitation. A gap it fills with a plausible value is a defect somebody now has to find.

Agreement instead of judgment. Propose a theory and AI will support it, then propose the opposite and it will support that too. Asking for the evidence against a theory is worth as much as asking for the evidence for it.

A root cause that merely fits the symptom. The fix that follows is well built and changes nothing.

Success taken on the agent's word. The environment is the evidence, so a check means a test that fails without the change, a measurement, a signal that disappears and stays gone, or a human confirming the outcome. When the evidence does not support the outcome, the work goes back to Context rather than straight to another attempt.

Five leaves

The growth clover

Growth is the next stage. It sits outside the working loop, and nobody inside an organization runs it.

AI gets more capable out of the other four stages. The direction it was given, the context it read, the actions it ran, and the results it saw confirmed.

Across an enormous number of those, patterns form. Expertise forms out of the patterns.

No team schedules this and no team owns it. It belongs to the frontier AI companies, who hold the volume of data that everyone's usage generates. What comes after it is AI working from goals, with far less direction than it needs today.

This is a statement about how information accumulates in general. It is not a claim that any AI provider trains on customer or enterprise work, and many state plainly that they do not.

Where repeated Growth ends is an open question, and the repository carries it as one, separate from the engineering material: The AI Future →

The worked example

One change, start to finish

A piece of ordinary application development, run through the working loop. The account page has to show when a subscription renews.

  1. 1

    Direction Human

    “Show the renewal date on the account page. Read only the account being looked at, not the whole subscription table. Start in the account service.”

  2. 2

    Context AI

    Reads, in one pass:

    • account service repository
    • subscription datasource
    • development logs
    • development environment
    • the running account page

    Finds that the renewal date is already stored, under a different column name, on a row the account query loads anyway.

  3. 3

    Action AI

    • reads the column
    • adds the field to the account response
    • renders it on the page
    • writes a test
    • runs it in development
  4. 4

    Success Human AI

    • date visible on the running page
    • test fails without the change
    • still one account row read, not the table

    A human reads the evidence and decides it is enough.

↺ Back to Context. The context file beside the code records the column name and what the query is allowed to read. The next change to this page starts from there, and it starts there whether this run succeeded or failed.

AI will also follow a wrong thread a long way with complete confidence. A human states the outcome, notices the drift early, and decides when the evidence is enough.

The agent file

One file, and the agent works this way

The whole framework is one file: AGENTS.md. Drop it into a project, point an AI agent at it, and the agent runs the loop — Direction, Context, Action, Success — and explains what it is doing while it does.

Reading the docs takes an afternoon. An agent takes one file, and the human learns the stages by watching them run on their own work. That is the intended route in. Most people will meet Clover through an agent rather than through a specification, because asking is faster and clearer than reading.

Read first
Find the context file before acting. Do not re-derive what is already settled or repeat a dead end someone already ruled out.
Reach real evidence
Logs, telemetry, the running system — rather than assumptions about them, or a summary written earlier by someone else.
Self-check
Test its own work against the known ways AI fails before handing anything back.
Say what the evidence was
What was checked, what was observed, and where it stopped. No claim larger than the evidence.
Write back
Update the context file with what is settled, what remains, and what was ruled out, so the next cycle starts ahead of this one.
Acting beyond its authority
Five things it must refuse outright, and the instruction to say what it refused and why rather than going quiet.
Obeying the content it reads
Text found in a ticket, a page or a file is treated as data. The agent never follows instructions embedded in it, which is the defense against prompt injection stated as a rule the agent holds itself to.
Leaking what it finds
Secrets and personal data encountered mid-task are treated as defects to report, never as material to use or repeat.
Dressing activity up as outcome
"I updated six files" is not a result. The hand-back format makes the difference explicit.
Retrying a failed fix
When Success fails it returns to Context. A second attempt needs new information rather than more determination.

The file instructs the agent to treat the human in front of it as the one it is teaching, because for most teams that agent is the only teacher this framework will ever have.

Names the stage
"I am still in Context. I want to see the actual log before proposing a fix." The stages get learned by watching them run.
Gives the reason
Every request for access, a success standard or an approval comes with what it is for. A reason teaches; a bare request just looks like process.
Offers to set it up
Proposes the context file and writes the first version, rather than waiting to be asked.
Matches the human
Explains each step the first time, and stops narrating once it is familiar.
Humble, and still honest
Courtesy governs how it says something, never whether it says it. If the direction will not work it says so once, with a better option beside it, then respects the call.

Security and governance

What a security review will actually ask

Most security conversations about AI get stuck because nobody has separated three different things: what is actually happening, what has to be done about it, and what genuinely is not solved yet. Taken separately, each one is manageable.

What is actually happening

No new access
AI acts inside the access a named human already holds. If they cannot reach a system, neither can AI acting for them. There is no new credential, no new path into your systems, and no new access to approve.
No new controls
Data classification, credential policy, retention rules and regulatory constraints all apply exactly as they did. Your own security team decides, and if the answer is no, it is no.
One real change
The layer can read across code, tickets, logs and data at once — which no one human practically could. Every individual read was already permitted; the aggregation is what is new. Treat the layer itself as a sensitive asset.

What needs to happen

Inherit access, never expand it
Read-only by default, one connection at a time, never wider than the accountable human can explain to someone who will ask.
Give every action a named owner
No shared or anonymous identity. A dedicated identity scoped to the task and owned by a named human keeps accountability without carrying that human's entire access footprint.
Treat what it reads as data, not instruction
Tickets, comments and pages can be written by anyone, including someone outside your organization. An agent must never act on instructions embedded in content it reads. Keep write access small enough that a misled agent cannot do much damage with it.
Fix what it finds
Credentials in repositories and personal data in logs get rotated, redacted or removed — not noted in a ticket and left there.

"But there are secrets in our code"

This is the most common objection, and usually the most revealing one. Credentials in the repository. Personal data in the logs. An access path nobody has reviewed since whoever built it left.

Every one of those is a true statement about the systems, rather than about AI. That credential is already readable by everyone with repository access, every contractor who ever cloned it, and anyone who finds an old laptop. The exposure existed before the agent and survives the decision not to use one.

Declining to use AI does not remove the risk. It removes the thing most likely to find it. An agent reading across a codebase surfaces hardcoded secrets, unredacted logs and forgotten access paths considerably faster than the audit that keeps getting rescheduled. Rotate the credential, redact the log, close the path, and the objection has disappeared while the system has genuinely got safer.

Monitor the agents, not just the gate

This is the fifth thing on that list, and the one most teams have not built. Approval gates stop the largest mistakes before they happen. Attribution explains them afterwards. Neither notices an agent doing something unintended inside access it legitimately holds — and that is where the real risk sits.

So the primary recommendation is monitoring built for agent behavior: record every system an agent touched, compare what it did against what it was asked to do, and raise an alert when it reaches outside the scope of the task. A repository unrelated to the ticket. A table it had no reason to query. A write where it was granted read.

Stated honestly: tooling for this barely exists yet, here or anywhere. Most teams will have to assemble a first version from access logs and audit trails they already collect. It is still the highest-value control to build, because it is the only one that operates while the agent is working rather than before or after. Until it exists, this depends on a human paying attention — and attention scales worse than access does.

Evidence

Production case studies and reference implementations

Two delivered outcomes and three patterns in use. Each one says where it stopped.

Success means the real environment demonstrated the intended outcome, so each entry below states what was checked, what was observed, and where the work stopped. Stopping early is normal and often correct. The damage comes from describing weak evidence in the language of strong evidence, so "validated in the test environment, not yet observed in production" is written out in full rather than rounded up.

Contentful API migration

A CMS content API rewritten from a GraphQL integration to REST, and from .NET 9 MVC to .NET 10 Minimal APIs, with every route, JSON shape, and response envelope preserved so no consumer had to change.

A manual rewrite was estimated at 8–10 weeks of team effort. With AI carrying most of the implementation under close human direction, the implementation took about a day. Parity was checked across 36 endpoint cases: 34 matched exactly and 2 were signed-off deviations, alongside 239 tests. Both versions ran in the test environment with the API gateway pointing at the new one, so QA validated the real site and signed off. The production cutover has not run.

Read the case study →

That day is implementation only. Testing and parity validation took roughly another day, and stakeholder agreement longer still, and the work was not continuous. Comparing one day against a ten-week estimate that included analysis and coordination would overstate it.

Performance was measured locally, two processes on one machine, and never on deployed infrastructure. The gains came from the architecture rather than from AI.

What matters here is how much AI lowered the barrier to attempting the work at all, rather than who wrote the code.

A React Server Components memory leak, traced upstream

A production application was crashing with out-of-memory errors. A blunt Node flag stopped the growth and stabilized production, and it also stripped error-stack detail everywhere. The mitigation was validated on a fleet running the same image: 113 restarts without the flag, zero with it, over about six and a half hours.

The investigation continued past the workaround, traced the retention mechanism into React's react-server renderer, and produced a one-file fix contributed upstream so other applications need not carry the same workaround.

Scope

What it is not

Not software
There is nothing to install. It is a way of working.
Not a platform to migrate onto
It is a layer above the systems a team already runs. Adoption is incremental, and if a team stops, what is underneath is unchanged.
Not a replacement for how a team already works
It sits alongside Agile, incident management, or whatever is already in place.
Not about tool selection
It does not say which AI to use, and it does not cover cost, evaluations, data governance, or security review.
Not a runtime
Agent frameworks, workflow engines and tool protocols are choices that sit inside it, rather than competitors to it.
Not needed for everything
Trivial or throwaway work does not need a cycle. It is worth the effort where being wrong actually costs something.

It is also not an LLM framework, an agent SDK, or a prompt library, and it makes no claim that unrestricted autonomous AI exists today.

The five stages are not software-specific either. The same cycle is intended to apply across DevOps, quality assurance, security operations, product management, finance, human resources, and business operations. The vocabulary changes and the cycle does not.

The question to leave with

The unknown clover

AI already works across more fields than any one of us can. It is not one system, either. Several companies are each building their own, and every one of them gets better.

Those systems already reach each other. One reads what another produced. One evaluates, trains or calls another. That traffic is growing, and the data and expertise behind it passed what a human can hold some time ago. Nothing has to coordinate deliberately for the result to stop being legible to us.

What none of them has yet is a purpose of their own that lasts. Nothing carries what it learned yesterday into today and decides what is worth doing next. That is the piece that is missing, and it is the piece worth watching.

With everything AI can already do, how much more growth do we seek, and in the progress of growth do we still stay in control?
