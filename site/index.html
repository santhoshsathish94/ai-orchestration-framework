Clover

Clover is a five-leaf model for doing real work with AI inside it. It describes how human intent becomes an outcome the real environment confirms, and what the system keeps once it does.

  1. Direction
  2. Context
  3. Action
  4. Success
  5. Growth
Direction
A person says which outcome is worth reaching, and what stays out of the work.
Context
The system reads the real environment before it acts on it.
Action
The work gets planned and done inside boundaries somebody stated.
Success
The environment shows that the intended outcome happened.
Growth
What the cycle taught gets written where the next one will read it.

Short form: Where → Know → Do → Validate → Become.

Growth is not a finish line. What one cycle keeps becomes the Context the next one starts from, it changes how Action gets planned, and it sometimes changes the Direction, because the work showed that a different outcome was the one worth having.

The problem this solves

Where the work usually breaks down

Most teams already have the capability. Skilled people, and now capable AI. What is missing is the part around it: knowing what the work is for, seeing the real system, doing the work inside boundaries, checking the result against reality, and keeping what the cycle taught.

The same pattern shows up at every level.

  • A person acts before understanding the problem, and the fix misses.
  • A team adds people to go faster, context travels badly, and the work turns into handoff and rework.
  • An organization gives everyone AI, and the skill stays with a few people instead of becoming shared capability.
  • An agent jumps to a fix and thrashes, because what was missing was information rather than capability.

AI does not create that gap. It makes the gap easier to see, because AI acts on missing context faster and more confidently than a person does.

Each of the five leaves below is one of those parts, with what commonly goes wrong at it.

Leaf 1 · Direction

Where are we going, and what outcome are we trying to achieve?

Direction is where human intent enters the system. A person decides what is worth doing, why it matters, which outcome counts, which constraints apply, and what should stay out of the work.

A ticket can trigger it. Direction is the outcome behind the ticket, said plainly, with the edges of the work attached. A direction that says where to go and never says where not to gets read generously by whoever picks it up.

Who holds what

Human
The objective, the priorities, the constraints, the risk boundaries, what counts as a good outcome, and what stays out of scope. Ownership of the outcome stays with a person after the work is handed over.
AI
Sharpens it. Restates the objective in its own words, asks what should happen to the parts nobody mentioned, points out where two stated goals conflict, and turns a vague request into something specific enough to work from.

People do not drop out of the work

Today

Human

Goal · constraints · path · decisions

AI

Assistance · execution

Where results have held

Human

Objective · constraints · authority · risk boundaries · what counts as success

AI

Context gathering · planning · execution · observation · adaptation

A team moves from the first column toward the second by earning it, per context rather than everywhere at once. Widen what AI determines where outcomes of that kind have repeatedly held up. Keep human approval wherever a mistake is expensive or hard to reverse. Narrow it again the moment the results stop holding. How that is held in practice →

What goes wrong here

Direction gets skipped, because a ticket looks like enough. The work then optimizes for closing the ticket rather than for the outcome.

Scope goes unstated. Nobody said the change must not touch payments, so nothing stopped it.

Constraints given once in a long session quietly stop being honored. Boundaries belong somewhere durable — the brief, the repository, the ticket — rather than only in the conversation.

Leaf 2 · Context

What do we need to know about reality before acting?

Context is what the system needs to know about reality before acting. Source code, documentation, architecture, real data, logs, runtime state, tests, how the system actually behaves, earlier attempts, and memory carried from previous cycles.

Prompting is a small part of this. Where the environment can answer a question, we read the environment rather than reasoning about what it probably contains. Collecting everything is its own failure, because a context packed with irrelevant material buries the few facts the problem turns on.

Who holds what

Human
Access and judgment. Which systems may be read, which data may be used, which sources can be trusted, and when the context in hand is good enough to act on.
AI
Most of the gathering. Reading code, tracing a call path, pulling telemetry, reconstructing an incident from logs, finding the earlier attempt nobody remembered. It also reports what it could not reach, and that part matters most.

A gap an agent names is a limitation. A gap it fills with a plausible guess is a defect somebody now has to find. Context engineering →

What goes wrong here

The plausible answer. A model working from thin context produces something that reads correctly and describes a system that does not exist. Searching for the exact string it cited usually settles it.

A root cause that merely fits the symptom. The fix that follows is well built and changes nothing.

Agreement instead of judgment. Propose a theory and AI will support it, then propose the opposite and it will support that too. Asking for the evidence against a theory is worth as much as asking for the evidence for it.

Hoarding. A context stuffed with everything reachable is harder to reason from than a context with the right few things in it.

Leaf 3 · Action

What should we do, and how should the work happen?

Action covers deciding how the work should happen and then doing it. Reasoning, planning, tool and model choice, orchestration across agents, code changes, tests, debugging, and interaction with whatever environments the work touches.

Planning and doing sit on one leaf on purpose. The plan usually changes once the work meets reality, and keeping the two together makes that change ordinary rather than an exception to explain.

Who holds what

Human
The boundaries and the approvals. Which decisions need a person before they happen, what may run unattended, and who owns each part when the work is split. Delegation moves the work and leaves accountability where it was.
AI
Most of the work, inside those boundaries. Choosing an approach, sequencing the steps, picking tools, running subagents where parts are genuinely independent, implementing, testing, and coming back when the evidence contradicts the plan.

Spend capability where it counts

A strong model holds the plan and owns the outcome. Cheaper models do scoped work beneath it as subagents, and the owning model checks and corrects what comes back. They are supervised rather than trusted.

That costs far less than running everything at the top tier, and it produces better results than running everything at the bottom, because accountability stays in one place. At one person's scale the saving is modest. Across an organization it compounds, and so does the waste if nobody looks at it. How to divide the work →

What goes wrong here

Thrash. A change fails, the next attempt is a variation of the same change, and each one sounds as confident as the last. The second failed attempt is the signal to stop and go back to Context.

Misplaced parallelism. Splitting work that shares state costs more in coordination and rework than it saves.

Motion read as progress. AI removes the effort cost of starting work and leaves the attention cost of directing it, so human attention stays the scarce resource.

Leaf 4 · Success

Did reality validate the intended outcome?

Success means the real environment demonstrates the intended outcome. The environment is the evidence. An AI reporting that it worked, a plausible explanation, a confident tone, and a high model score all sit outside that.

Evidence can be a test that fails without the change and passes with it, a before-and-after measurement, telemetry, a non-production run, a production signal that disappears and stays gone, or a user confirming the outcome.

Say what the evidence actually is

Not every task has telemetry, and pretending otherwise makes this leaf unusable for most work. What matters is describing what was actually done to check, in words a reader can act on. Four questions do the work.

  • Did anyone verify it, or is someone asserting it?
  • Does it hold up again?
  • Did the thing we cared about move?
  • Did it hold where it counts?

State what you checked, what you observed, and where you stopped. Stopping early is fine and often correct, because a small change can be genuinely complete once a test covers it and production observation is not always available or worth its cost. What causes damage is describing weak evidence in the language of strong evidence. "Validated in the test environment, not yet observed in production" is a complete and honest claim.

When Success fails

A failed check is a normal outcome. When the evidence does not support the intended outcome, the work returns to Context rather than to Action. A second attempt on the same information reaches the same place, faster. It needs something new: what the environment did instead, which assumption broke, which signal nobody had looked at yet. Success in practice →

Who holds what

Human
The standard. What counts as sufficient evidence for this work, and what risk is acceptable if the answer turns out to be wrong. Approval for anything expensive or hard to reverse stays with a person.
AI
Runs the checks, gathers the evidence, and reports it accurately, including what did not work and what it could not check.

What goes wrong here

Evidence described more strongly than it is. "Verified" covering a single manual look. A merged change reported as a resolved problem.

Reported success that nobody ran. Describing a completed task and completing it are similar acts for a language model, so the artifact is worth more than the claim.

Stopping at the artifact. The build passed, the work is treated as done, and nobody checks whether the original signal changed.

Leaf 5 · Growth

What did the system become or learn?

Growth is what the system accumulates across repeated cycles: persistent memory, experience, learned patterns, expertise, better planning, better tool choice, more capability than it had before.

The model does not do this by remembering. A session ends and its working memory goes with it. What makes the difference is plain markdown files committed alongside the code, written while the work happens rather than at the end.

What the file holds

The goal
What outcome is being pursued, and what done means.
Established
Findings that are settled, and the evidence behind each.
Remaining
Open questions, unverified assumptions, next steps.
Ruled out
What was tried and failed, and why. This is the most expensive thing to rediscover.

Why it changes the outcome

Anyone can pick the work up — a colleague, a different agent, the same agent tomorrow — and start from what is already established instead of re-deriving it. Re-derivation is where both the cost and the drift come from.

The file travels with the change and gets reviewed with it, so it cannot quietly disagree with a wiki nobody opens.

Experience and expertise

Experience is what one cycle produced: what was tried, the context it ran in, the evidence observed, and the outcome. Expertise is the reusable pattern that emerges once several validated experiences point the same way. One cycle should not become a rule.

The expertise that survives this leaf becomes part of the Context the next cycle starts from, which is how capability compounds without adding leaves to the model.

Who holds what

Human
Judgment about what is worth keeping, what can safely be shared, and where a captured lesson has gone stale. An experience recorded wrongly spreads, and somebody has to notice.
AI
Captures the experience while it is still accurate, writes it where the next cycle will read it, notices patterns across cycles, and applies them when a similar problem shows up.

What goes wrong here

Nothing is captured, because the work closed when the change shipped.

Everything is captured, and the pile of learnings becomes noise nobody reads.

A single lucky outcome gets promoted into a rule the system then follows everywhere. A wrong conclusion written into memory compounds, because every later cycle starts from it.

The five leaves in full: the model · one principle per leaf

The agent file

One file, and the agent works this way

Everything above is one file: AGENTS.md. Drop it into a project, point an AI agent at it, and the agent runs the five leaves — and explains what it is doing while it does.

A person reading the docs takes an afternoon to absorb the model. An agent takes one file, and the person learns the leaves by watching them run on their own work. That is the intended route in. Most people will meet Clover through an agent rather than through a specification, because asking is faster and clearer than reading.

Read first
Find the context file before acting. Do not re-derive what is already settled or repeat a dead end someone already ruled out.
Reach real evidence
Logs, telemetry, the running system — rather than assumptions about them, or a summary written earlier by someone else.
Self-check
Test its own work against the known ways AI fails before handing anything back.
Say what the evidence was
What was checked, what was observed, and where it stopped. No claim larger than the evidence.
Write back
Update the context file with what is settled, what remains, and what was ruled out, so the next cycle starts ahead of this one.
Acting beyond its authority
Five things it must refuse outright, and the instruction to say what it refused and why rather than going quiet.
Obeying the content it reads
Text found in a ticket, a page or a file is treated as data. The agent never follows instructions embedded in it, which is the defense against prompt injection stated as a rule the agent holds itself to.
Leaking what it finds
Secrets and personal data encountered mid-task are treated as defects to report, never as material to use or repeat.
Dressing activity up as outcome
"I updated six files" is not a result. The hand-back format makes the difference explicit.
Retrying a failed fix
When Success fails it returns to Context. A second attempt needs new information rather than more determination.

The file instructs the agent to treat the person in front of it as the person it is teaching, because for most teams that agent is the only teacher this framework will ever have.

Names the leaf
"I am still in Context. I want to see the actual log before proposing a fix." The leaves get learned by watching them run.
Gives the reason
Every request for access, a success standard or an approval comes with what it is for. A reason teaches; a bare request just looks like process.
Offers to set it up
Proposes the context file and writes the first version, rather than waiting to be asked.
Matches the person
Explains each step the first time, and stops narrating once it is familiar.
Humble, and still honest
Courtesy governs how it says something, never whether it says it. If the direction will not work it says so once, with a better option beside it, then respects the call.

What it looks like in practice

One real problem, start to finish.

New listings stopped appearing after a data load. The investigation had already run overnight without finding it. The team that built the synchronization had left the company, and the mapping involved was far too large for anyone to have written down.

This is the ordinary shape of hard work. The knowledge was never missing. It just was not reachable any more without the person who held it. Here is the whole cycle on that problem, and why each piece of access earns its place.

What it reads, and what each thing answers

The repository
Where the sync is actually implemented, which is rarely where the documentation says it is.
Work tracking
What changed recently, and what the overnight investigation already ruled out — so none of it is repeated.
Logs and telemetry
Which step ran, which returned nothing, and which failed quietly without raising anything.
The datastore
What the data looks like right now, rather than what the schema implies it should look like.
A non-production environment
Somewhere to reproduce the behavior without touching a customer.
The running application
What the customer is actually seeing, which is where the whole thing started.

None of that is exotic. It is the same evidence an experienced engineer would open, in roughly the same order. What changes is that it can be read together, in one pass, by something that has not been away from this area for two years.

The five leaves, and who holds what

Direction
Human Says what a good outcome is: listings appear again after a load, rather than "go and look at the sync job". Naming the outcome instead of the task is what stops the work drifting into activity.
Context
AI Works backwards from the customer-visible symptom through the application, the logs and the data to the source. This is the part that used to need the person who left.
Action
Human AI The cause turns out to be a single misconfigured flag between ingestion and the database. A person decides the smallest change that fixes it and who approves it. AI makes the change and raises it through the review and deployment path the team already uses.
Success
Human Listings appear after the next load, and keep appearing. The original signal is gone and stays gone, and a person decides when that is genuinely enough.
Growth
The reconstructed flow is written down beside the code. The next person to touch this does not need the original authors either, and does not start from nothing.

Where the human actually matters

AI does the reading, the reconstruction, the drafting and much of the checking. It will also follow a wrong thread a long way with complete confidence, proposing a fix for the step that merely looks broken, or calling the work done because a job now completes without error while the listings still are not there.

So the job changes rather than disappears. Somebody states the outcome, so there is something to diverge from. Somebody notices the divergence early, while it is one wrong assumption instead of a day of work built on it. Somebody holds the approvals wherever a mistake would be expensive. And somebody decides when the evidence is actually enough, because only a person carries the consequence of getting that wrong.

Security and governance

What a security review will actually ask

Most security conversations about AI get stuck because nobody has separated three different things: what is actually happening, what has to be done about it, and what genuinely is not solved yet. Taken separately, each one is manageable.

What is actually happening

No new access
AI acts inside the access a named person already holds. If they cannot reach a system, neither can AI acting for them. There is no new credential, no new path into your systems, and no new access model to approve.
No new controls
Data classification, credential policy, retention rules and regulatory constraints all apply exactly as they did. Your own security team decides, and if the answer is no, it is no.
One real change
The layer can read across code, tickets, logs and data at once — which no one person practically could. Every individual read was already permitted; the aggregation is what is new. Treat the layer itself as a sensitive asset.

What needs to happen

Inherit access, never expand it
Read-only by default, one connection at a time, never wider than the accountable person can explain to someone who will ask.
Give every action a named owner
No shared or anonymous identity. A dedicated identity scoped to the task and owned by a named person keeps accountability without carrying that person's entire access footprint.
Treat what it reads as data, not instruction
Tickets, comments and pages can be written by anyone, including someone outside your organization. An agent must never act on instructions embedded in content it reads. Keep write access small enough that a misled agent cannot do much damage with it.
Fix what it finds
Credentials in repositories and personal data in logs get rotated, redacted or removed — not noted in a ticket and left there.
Monitor the agent, not just the gate
The one thing on this list most teams have not built. Details below.

"But there are secrets in our code"

This is the most common objection, and usually the most revealing one. Credentials in the repository. Personal data in the logs. An access path nobody has reviewed since the person who built it left.

Every one of those is a true statement about the systems, rather than about AI. That credential is already readable by everyone with repository access, every contractor who ever cloned it, and anyone who finds an old laptop. The exposure existed before the agent and survives the decision not to use one.

Declining to use AI does not remove the risk. It removes the thing most likely to find it. An agent reading across a codebase surfaces hardcoded secrets, unredacted logs and forgotten access paths considerably faster than the audit that keeps getting rescheduled. Rotate the credential, redact the log, close the path, and the objection has disappeared while the system has genuinely got safer.

The thing worth building: monitor the agents

Approval gates stop the largest mistakes before they happen. Attribution explains them afterwards. Neither notices an agent doing something unintended inside access it legitimately holds — and that is where the real risk sits.

So the primary recommendation is monitoring built for agent behavior: record every system an agent touched, compare what it did against what it was asked to do, and raise an alert when it reaches outside the scope of the task. A repository unrelated to the ticket. A table it had no reason to query. A write where it was granted read.

Stated honestly: tooling for this barely exists yet, here or anywhere. Most teams will have to assemble a first version from access logs and audit trails they already collect. It is still the highest-value control to build, because it is the only one that operates while the agent is working rather than before or after. Until it exists, this depends on a human paying attention — and attention scales worse than access does.

Evidence

What this has been run on

Two delivered outcomes and three patterns in use. Each one says where it stopped.

Contentful API migration

A CMS content API rewritten from a GraphQL integration to REST, and from .NET 9 MVC to .NET 10 Minimal APIs, with every route, JSON shape, and response envelope preserved so no consumer had to change.

A manual rewrite was estimated at 8–10 weeks of team effort. With AI carrying most of the implementation under close human direction, the implementation took about a day. Parity was checked across 36 endpoint cases: 34 matched exactly and 2 were signed-off deviations, alongside 239 tests. Both versions ran in the test environment with the API gateway pointing at the new one, so QA validated the real site and signed off. The production cutover has not run.

That day is implementation only. Testing and parity validation took roughly another day, and stakeholder agreement longer still, and the work was not continuous. Comparing one day against a ten-week estimate that included analysis and coordination would overstate it.

Performance was measured locally, two processes on one machine, and never on deployed infrastructure. The gains came from the architecture rather than from AI.

The point is not who wrote the code. It is how much AI lowered the barrier to attempting the work at all.

Read the case study →

A React Server Components memory leak, traced upstream

A production application was crashing with out-of-memory errors. A blunt Node flag stopped the growth and stabilized production, and it also stripped error-stack detail everywhere. The mitigation was validated on a fleet running the same image: 113 restarts without the flag, zero with it, over about six and a half hours.

The investigation continued past the workaround, traced the retention mechanism into React's react-server renderer, and produced a one-file fix contributed upstream so other applications need not carry the same workaround.

Scope

What it is not

Not software
There is nothing to install. It is a way of working.
Not a platform to migrate onto
It is a layer above the systems a team already runs. Adoption is incremental, and if a team stops, what is underneath is unchanged.
Not a replacement for how a team already works
It sits alongside Agile, incident management, or whatever is already in place.
Not about model or tool selection
It does not say which AI to use, and it does not cover cost, evaluations, data governance, or security review.
Not a runtime
Agent frameworks, workflow engines and tool protocols are choices that sit inside it, rather than competitors to it.
Not needed for everything
Trivial or throwaway work does not need a cycle. It is worth the effort where being wrong actually costs something.

It is also not an LLM framework, an agent SDK, or a prompt library, and it makes no claim that unrestricted autonomous AI exists today.

The five leaves are not software-specific either. The same cycle is intended to apply across DevOps, quality assurance, security operations, product management, finance, human resources, and business operations. The vocabulary changes and the cycle does not.

The fifth leaf

The open question

Hypothesis, not prediction

Everything above is meant to be usable today. This part is a question nobody can answer yet, and it is kept separate on purpose.

Every cycle produces Growth. Growth persists, it compounds, and it makes the next cycle more capable than the last. Nobody knows where that ends.

Questions worth asking:

  • What happens when growth is shared across many systems rather than kept inside one.
  • What happens when it is embodied, so a system learns from the physical world.
  • What happens when a system starts influencing or selecting its own Direction rather than executing the one it was given.

None of that is claimed as established, and none of it is described here as inevitable. Growth is useful, beneficial and adaptive today. The open part is how far it goes.

Direction asks where we should go. Growth asks what we become along the way.

The name

The five-leaf clover is the framework's identity. It is borrowed from Black Clover, where a fifth leaf carries a devil association. Clover reuses the symbol and not the meaning. Here the fifth leaf stands for the unknown boundary of Growth, which is uncertainty rather than danger.

The longer version lives in the repository, labeled as a hypothesis: The AI Future → It is a thought experiment and it is not part of the engineering model.
